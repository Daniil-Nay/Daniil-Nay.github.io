Abstract TWIG represents neural network weights as shared discrete decision paths, unifying compression methods and enabling efficient fixed-weight circuits with optimized discrete choices.  Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small We introduce TWIG (Tree-structured Weights with Inherited Gates), a representation in which every weight of a neural network is the end of a path of small discrete decisions: a sign, a shift by a power of two, a zero gate, an index into a shared table. Weights that belong together share the upper part of their paths, and each decision is inherited by every weight below it, so it is stored once and applied once. This simple change has three consequences. Any trained network can be written as a TWIG, and when the decisions are powers of two its weight layers become circuits made almost entirely of wires and adders, suited to chips whose weights are fixed at fabrication. Pruning, quantization, low-rank factorization, codebooks and weight sharing become edits of one object, so a single optimizer can choose among all of them for a whole network. And choosing the decisions is the ground-state problem of a higher-order Boltzmann machine whose interactions follow the paths of the tree, the problem class that annealers and quantum optimizers are built to solve. Today these questions are handled apart: each compression method brings its own format and solver, a circuit with fixed weights needs a dedicated constant multiplier for every weight, and discrete solvers reach only narrow subproblems such as rounding. Weight formats have long been moving toward shared decisions, through group scales, shared exponents, microexponents and sparse weights; TWIG takes this trend to its end, and every common format is a shallow TWIG at no extra cost. On five weight matrices of a large language model, a flat tree whose signed power-of-two terms are chosen per weight for circuit cost, with no group scales, needs 9--15\% fewer additions than GPTQ at equal layer error at 3- and 4-bit accuracy on typical matrices, and 20--55\% fewer on matrices with strong outliers.

Abstract DeCert evaluates and improves generative reward model rationales by assessing justification validity and verdict consistency without reference explanations, yielding stronger preference supervision.  Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small Generative reward models (GenRMs) generate natural language rationales to justify preference judgments, yet a correct verdict does not guarantee a sound justification. Existing approaches often rely on reference explanations to supervise rationales, but obtaining such references can be costly and does not necessarily establish whether generated rationales support the resulting verdict. To address these limitations, we propose DeCert, a framework that evaluates the validity and verdict consistency of generated rationales without requiring reference explanations. Crucially, DeCert translates these assessments into preference supervision that accounts for both verdict correctness and justification quality. In particular, when multiple sampled judgments share the same correct verdict, differences in certificate quality enable DeCert to construct rationale preference pairs that distinguish stronger from weaker justifications. Extensive empirical studies across two model families and multiple in- and out-of-distribution benchmarks demonstrate that DeCert consistently outperforms strong generative judge baselines while improving rationale quality. Moreover, DeCert effectively mitigates positional bias, improving position-consistent accuracy by up to 14.8 points. These results highlight the importance of supervising not only which verdict is correct, but also how that verdict is justified.

Abstract Graph soft prompting in LLMs suffers from token collapse, where learned graph tokens become indistinguishable and function as generic prompts rather than conveying relational structure, prompting evaluation via alignment probes rather than accuracy alone.  Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small Recent methods for injecting graph structure into LLM prompts include graph soft prompting, in which a graph neural network (GNN) learns graph-specific tokens that capture relational information among entities in textual prompts. G-Retriever combines graph soft prompts with a verbalized description of the same graph to strengthen the textual prompt signal. We conduct an in-depth analysis of G-Retriever and uncover a pathological behavior we term token collapse. Across three benchmarks, three graph encoders, two LLM families, and both frozen and LoRA-adapted models, G-Retriever's graph tokens become almost indistinguishable across inputs. We provide evidence that the same failure is inherited by subsequent methods, even with multi-token pooling. Under the standard setup, swapping a token with the dataset mean, a token from another example, or one derived from a corrupted graph produces no resolved answer-level effect in accuracy. Moreover, a learned constant prompt that never accesses the graph matches or surpasses G-Retriever on all three benchmarks. These findings indicate that, rather than conveying graph content as intended, the collapsed token functions primarily as a generic soft prompt. We also examine conditions linked to this collapse and propose a two-stage procedure that yields non-collapsed tokens while maintaining competitive performance in most cases. However, token variability alone does not prove that the model uses graph information: via interventions and decoding probes, we show that depending on the configuration, a token can reflect the question, an answer prior, or graph-derived content that the LLM may or may not use. Overall, our results offer a practical approach to evaluating graph-token alignment rather than inferring it from task accuracy alone. Code to reproduce our experiments is available at https://github.com/difra100/Token_Collapse_KGQA.

Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small Vision-language-action (VLA) models have become an important paradigm for robotic manipulation by unifying visual perception, language understanding, and action generation. However, our experiments show that existing VLA models do not fully exploit such multi-view complementarity. The policy tends to persistently rely on the wrist view, lacking the ability to dynamically adjust global-local view dependency across manipulation stages, which limits stage adaptability and robustness. To address this issue, we propose FocusVLA, a stage-aware multi-view routing framework. FocusVLA predicts future interaction intent from historical actions and uses it to guide dynamic view routing during action generation. By adaptively coordinating global and local observations across different task stages, FocusVLA improves multi-view utilization. On RoboTwin 2.0, FocusVLA improves the average success rate over the strongest baseline π_{0.5} by 10.10 pp. In real-world evaluation, FocusVLA improves the success rate from 71.7\% to 85.0\%, achieving a 13.3\% gain over π_{0.5}. Moreover, the intent prediction and dynamic routing modules of FocusVLA can be integrated into existing VLA frameworks without modifying their core architectures.
